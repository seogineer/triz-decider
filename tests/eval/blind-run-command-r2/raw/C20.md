## Contradiction definition
Making a wing adaptable to different flight conditions (morphing, variable geometry) makes it structurally weaker.

## Application ideas

### Principle #35 Parameter Changes (sub-principles: change its degree of flexibility, change its physical state, change its temperature)
- **Variable-stiffness structure.** Keep the wing stiff in cruise, when the loads are highest. Let it soften only during the phases where it needs to change shape. The stiffness can be switched by, for example, jamming layers or shape-memory alloy that changes stiffness with temperature.
- **Stiffness that follows the flight condition.** Set the wing's flexibility to the loading, so it is rigid in high-g manoeuvres and compliant in gentle ones. This means strength is only traded away when the wing isn't heavily loaded.

### Principle #3 Local Quality (sub-principles: differ from region to region, give each region a different function)
- **Rigid load-bearing box with a morphing outer part.** Keep the main spar and wing box as a conventional, strong structure. Restrict the adaptability to the leading edge, trailing edge or wingtip, where loads are lower. This keeps the strength-critical region free of joints.
- **Zoned design.** Use stiff composite in the load path and compliant material only in the areas that move.

### Principle #32 Color Changes (sub-principles: change how transparent the object is, add dyes or additives to make a hard-to-see object visible)
This principle fits the structural problem only loosely. Its use here is monitoring rather than a fix for the trade-off.
- **Damage visibility on morphing zones.** Use strain-sensitive or colour-changing coatings on hinges and flexible skin. Fatigue or overload then shows up in inspection, so the wing can be made lighter without losing the ability to catch weakening early.

### Principle #6 Universality (sub-principles: let one object perform several functions, gather the needed functions into a single object)
- **Structure that also does the adapting.** Use the wing skin or a compliant internal structure as both the load-bearing element and the shape-change mechanism. This removes separate actuators, linkages and hinged flaps, which are often the weak points.
- **Integrated actuators.** Embed actuation, such as piezo or shape-memory elements, in the structural material so the adapting function adds no extra joints.

### Principle #30 Flexible Shells and Thin Films (sub-principles: use flexible shells or thin films in place of bulky structures, isolate the object from its environment with a flexible membrane)
- **Flexible skin over a rigid frame.** Use an elastic membrane skin over the load-bearing frame, so the aerodynamic surface can change shape without cutting the structure with gaps or hinge lines.
- **Membrane seals at moving joints.** Cover the gaps at morphing sections with a flexible film. This limits the exposure of the joints to the environment and their wear.

## Next steps
- **Mapping to revisit.** I chose #13 as a second "worsen" candidate. If the real concern is fatigue life, #15 Duration of action (moving) may fit better. Re-running with #15 would check whether it changes the ranking.
- **Where the ideas point.** #35 and #3 are the strongest, because #35 appeared in both pairs. A combination of #3 and #35 is the most direct: a rigid box carrying the load, with variable-stiffness or morphing parts limited to the low-load regions.
- **How to check.** Compare candidate designs on a structural model, covering the maximum load case, fatigue cycles and the added mass of the morphing mechanism. Then confirm the leading option in a scaled wind-tunnel or ground-load test.
