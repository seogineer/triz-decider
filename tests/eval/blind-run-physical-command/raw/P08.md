## Contradiction Definition (Physical Contradiction)

The hardness of a drill bit's material must be **very high** in order to cut through steel, and it must be **low / more flexible (tough)** in order not to shatter under impact.

*(This restates hardness and toughness as opposite ends of the same "hard ↔ flexible/tough" scale, per the skill's physical-contradiction format — confirmed per the task instructions.)*

## Applied Ideas

### #1 Segmentation
- Build the bit as independent parts: a very hard cutting tip (e.g., carbide or diamond-coated insert) mechanically or metallurgically joined to a tougher steel shank, rather than making the whole bit from one material.
- Push the division finer: use a multi-layer edge (several thin hard layers) so that if one layer chips, the crack doesn't propagate through the whole edge.

### #2 Taking Out (Extraction)
- Extract "hardness" as a property and place it only where cutting actually happens — a thin hard coating (TiN, TiAlN, or a brazed carbide tip) — while the bulk of the bit, which only needs to transmit torque and absorb shock, is left as a tougher, more ductile steel.

### #3 Local Quality
- Make each region of the bit serve a different role: harden only the cutting edge and flutes via localized heat treatment (induction hardening, case hardening) or surface coating, while the shank and body retain their original (tougher, more ductile) microstructure.
- Vary hardness continuously along the bit's length — hardest at the tip, gradually softening toward the shank — so there's no abrupt hard/soft interface that could crack.

### #7 Nested Doll
- Nest a hard core inside a tougher outer structure (or vice versa): e.g., a tough steel core running through the bit for shock absorption, surrounded/tipped by a hard outer shell at the cutting zone, similar to how a hard carbide insert sits within a tougher tool-steel body.

### #4 Asymmetry
- Break structural symmetry so hardness and toughness aren't demanded identically everywhere: make the cutting-edge cross-section geometry different from the shank cross-section (e.g., thicker web/asymmetric flute design) so stresses from impact are directed away from the brittle hard zone toward the tougher body.
