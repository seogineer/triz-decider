## Contradiction Definition (Physical Contradiction)

The **passable direction/width of the single-lane mountain road** must be **wide enough for two-way traffic (bidirectional)** so that uphill and downhill vehicles can both move, and it must be **narrow / one-way only (unidirectional)** because the mountain terrain and construction cost only allow a single carriageway width.

## Applied Ideas

### #1 Segmentation
- Break the road into short, independent segments: keep most of the length single-lane, but insert periodic **passing bays/turnouts** where the road is briefly widened — the widened segments function independently of the narrow segments.
- Use assemble/disassemble modular widening panels or temporary barriers at pinch points so extra width can be added only during peak season and removed afterward.

### #2 Taking Out (Extraction)
- Pull the "waiting/stopped vehicle" function out of the through-lane entirely: build a small dedicated **pull-off apron** so a car yielding to oncoming traffic is physically removed from the travel lane rather than blocking it.
- Extract slow uphill trucks onto a separate short crawler lane at steep grades, leaving the main single lane clear for the opposing direction to flow.

### #3 Local Quality
- Vary the cross-section by location instead of uniformly: place the widened passing bay right after a blind curve (where visibility recovers), and keep the curve approach itself narrow and marked for single-file travel.
- Assign each zone a distinct role via signage/marking (e.g., "yield zone," "priority zone") so drivers know which local segment governs right-of-way.

### #7 Nested Doll
- Carve passing bays into pockets tucked within the existing hillside or retaining wall, rather than excavating a full-width road — the bay is "nested" inside the terrain, minimizing earthwork.
- Nest a small lay-by within a larger scenic overlook/parking area that already exists, so one construction footprint serves two purposes.

### #4 Asymmetry
- Give uphill traffic (heavier, slower-accelerating trucks) an asymmetric priority: a longer green phase at the signal-controlled ends, since starting and stopping loaded vehicles on a grade is costlier than for downhill traffic.
- Offset the road crown/shoulder asymmetrically (wider shoulder on the downhill side) so faster downhill vehicles have a recovery margin, while the uphill lane stays tighter against the hillside.
