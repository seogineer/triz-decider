## Application ideas
### #1 Segmentation
These ideas use the sub-principles "split into independently working parts" and "easy to assemble and take apart".
- Put each redundant valve in its own cartridge module with quick-connect ports. A failed valve can be swapped in minutes without opening the pump body.
- Use one standard cartridge for every valve position. This cuts the number of part types and makes assembly repetitive.
- Isolate each valve so a failure or a service job on one leaves the others running. The pump can then be serviced while it runs.

### #11 Beforehand Cushioning
This idea uses the sub-principle "prepare protective measures beforehand to make up for low reliability".
- Fit one standby valve that is normally dormant and takes over on failure, rather than parallel valves that are all active. There are fewer parts to build and less to maintain. Add a simple pressure or flow trigger for the switch-over.
- Keep spare cartridges on site, or pre-fitted in a dormant slot. This trades service effort for a spare part.

### #13 The Other Way Round
This idea uses the sub-principle "do the opposite of what the problem calls for".
- Instead of adding valves to make the pump reliable, make each valve more reliable, for example with a simpler or fail-safe design. That keeps the part count from growing. The reverse is also possible: put the redundancy outside the pump as a whole spare pump unit, so the pump itself stays simple to build.

### #35 Parameter Changes
This idea uses the sub-principle "change its degree of flexibility".
- Replace some of the mechanical valve redundancy with a flexible element, such as a compliant diaphragm or elastomer seat. It tolerates debris and wear better and needs fewer separate valves and fasteners.
